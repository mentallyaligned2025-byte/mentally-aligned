Why do people procrastinate?

Procrastination is often less about time and more about feelings. A task brings up worry, boredom, overwhelm or fear of getting it wrong, and putting it off gives quick relief.

The relief fades. The deadline doesn’t. So the loop repeats, usually with a layer of guilt on top. Understanding what you’re avoiding is the first step to changing it.

Common causes of procrastination

  • Perfectionism. If it can’t be done perfectly, it feels safer not to start.
  • Overwhelm. A big task with no clear first move is easy to circle forever.
  • Boredom. Nothing about the task gives your brain a reason to begin.
  • Low energy. Starting takes effort, and tiredness or stress makes everything heavier.
  • Anxiety. Worry about the outcome can make the task itself feel threatening.

How to stop procrastinating

  • Shrink the first step. Make it so small it takes about two minutes: open the document, write one sentence, find the email.
  • Make an if-then plan. Decide exactly when and where: “When it’s 4 pm, I’ll open the report and write one sentence.” A specific plan is easier to follow than a vague intention.
  • Use a timed block. Ten or fifteen minutes with your phone out of reach. You’re allowed to stop when the timer ends.
  • Aim to start, not to finish. Starting is the hardest part, and it usually gets easier once you’re in.

What is revenge bedtime procrastination?

It’s staying up late to get free time you didn’t get during the day. The need for time that belongs to you is real. One fix is to schedule a short stretch of it earlier in the evening, so bedtime isn’t the only place it lives.

Is procrastination a sign of ADHD or anxiety?

Everyone procrastinates sometimes. If it’s constant, hard to control, or affects your work, school or relationships, or comes with ongoing anxiety or low mood, a licensed professional can assess whether ADHD, anxiety or something else is involved. This page can’t tell you that.
